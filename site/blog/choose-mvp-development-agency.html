How to choose an MVP development agency for your startup

By Manish Saraan, Founder & Principal Engineer, SortedLaunch. 10+ years of engineering, 50+ MVPs launched.Published 31 October 2026

To choose an MVP development agency for your startup, get a named engineer you can talk to, a written scope with exclusions, a fixed price with a stated process for changes, a weekly demo of deployed software, launched products with founders who will take a call, and a contract that puts the code and every account in your name.

What should an MVP development agency actually deliver?

An MVP development agency for startups should deliver a working product that real users can pay for, built in weeks, structured so the next engineer can extend it, with the founder owning every line of code and every account. That is the whole job. Anything beyond it, such as a brand identity, a pitch deck, or a twelve-month roadmap, is a different purchase and should be priced as one.

The term MVP comes from Eric Ries, who defined it in The Lean Startup as the version of a product that lets a team collect the maximum amount of validated learning with the least effort. The definition matters when you hire, because it tells you what to refuse. A vendor who proposes a six-month build with every feature on your wishlist is not proposing an MVP. They are proposing a product, and a product built before anyone has paid for it is the most expensive way to learn that the idea needs changing.

I have built 50+ MVPs, first inside a 23-person agency I ran and now as a single senior engineer at SortedLaunch. The products behind those builds have secured $3M+ in funding, and I hold 30+ long-term retainers. I have also inherited many MVPs built elsewhere. The questions below come from both sides of that experience: what I looked for when I was buying engineering, and what went wrong on the projects that landed on my desk for repair.

Who will write the code?

Ask this first, and insist on a name. At many agencies the people on the sales call are not the people who build. The senior architect who impressed you scopes the project and reviews a pull request once a week, and the daily work goes to whoever is available. That is not dishonest, it is how agencies stay profitable, but it changes what you are buying.

Three follow-up questions tell you what you need to know. How many years has that person been building production web applications? Can you speak to them directly during the project, or does communication go through a project manager? And what happens if they leave the agency halfway through? The answer to the third one should include a specific plan, not reassurance.

When I ran the agency, the clients with the best outcomes were the ones who had a direct line to the engineer. That observation is why SortedLaunch is one person. It is also why I tell founders that a strong individual engineer with a track record is often a better purchase than a team, provided the scope fits in one person's weeks.

Is the scope written down, and is anything excluded?

A quote without a written scope is a quote for whatever the vendor decides to build. Before any money moves, you want a document that lists the screens, the user roles, the integrations, and the one core workflow the MVP must support end to end. Equally useful is the list of what is out: no mobile app in this phase, no multi-currency, no admin reporting.

The exclusions protect both sides. They stop the build growing while nobody is watching, and they tell you what the second phase will cost before you commit to the first. A vendor who refuses to write exclusions is telling you the scope will be decided during the project, which means it will be decided by whoever is loudest in the weekly call.

A reasonable MVP scope for a SaaS product fits on one page: authentication and account management, a billing integration, one workflow that delivers the value, a dashboard that shows the result, and the plumbing that makes it deployable. SortedLaunch builds that in 3 to 6 weeks, which is a useful calibration. If a vendor's estimate for a similar scope is three times longer, either the scope has grown or the team is slower, and you should find out which.

Is the price fixed, and what happens when the scope changes?

Hourly billing on an MVP moves the risk of a slow team onto the founder. Fixed pricing for a fixed scope moves it back to the vendor, where it belongs, because the vendor is the one who can estimate. Ask for a fixed price, and ask how changes are priced. A good answer is "each change is quoted as a small fixed addition before we do it". A bad answer is "we will track the hours".

Be wary of a fixed price that is dramatically lower than the others. It usually means a junior team, an offshore handoff with no senior oversight, or a scope that has been quietly narrowed. Ask what is not included and read the exclusions. Be equally wary of a quote that is dramatically higher than a single senior engineer would charge, unless the extra buys something you need, such as parallel design and mobile work.

For calibration, SortedLaunch full projects start at $3,500 at a fixed price agreed before work starts, and ongoing work runs at $3,000 per month with the option to pause or cancel. Agencies with larger teams will price higher, and that can be the right choice when the scope needs several people at once. The point of the comparison is to make you ask what the difference buys.

How often will you see working software?

In my experience on both sides, the strongest predictor of a good MVP project is how often the founder sees running software. Weekly is right. A vendor who shows you designs for a month and then code for a month has given you one chance to correct course, at the point where correcting it is most expensive.

Ask what the demo cadence is, whether the demo is of deployed software or a local screen share, and whether you get access to the staging environment between demos. The right answer is a deployed staging build you can click through yourself, updated at least weekly. SortedLaunch runs a demo every Friday because a founder who clicks through the product on Friday finds the misunderstanding on Friday, not at launch.

A related question: what does the first two weeks produce? A good vendor can describe a concrete pilot deliverable, a slice of the product that works end to end. A vendor whose first two weeks are "discovery and architecture" may be fine, but you are paying for a document rather than software, and you should know that going in.

Can they show launched products and name the people behind them?

Portfolios are easy to assemble and hard to verify. Ask for three products the team launched in the last two years, the URL of each, and a contact at each company who will take a call. A vendor with real launches will have founders willing to vouch for them. A vendor who offers only screenshots and NDA excuses may still be competent, but you are taking that on faith.

When you make those calls, ask the founder what went wrong, not only what went right. Every project has something. The useful signal is how the vendor handled it. For SortedLaunch, the case studies for BlinkIn, Blookery and LiveEasy are public, with the results stated, and the clients who have given testimonials are named on the about page. Ask any vendor you consider for the same.

Look at public code too. An engineer's GitHub shows how they write when nobody is paying. It does not need to be impressive. It needs to be readable, with commit messages that say what changed.

What will the codebase look like when they leave?

An MVP that works but cannot be changed is a dead end. The code will be inherited by you, your first hire, or another vendor, and it needs to be in a state they can work with. Ask for specifics: which framework and why, how the database schema is managed, whether there are automated tests on the core workflow, how deployment works, and whether there is a README that lets a new engineer run the project in an afternoon.

The answers do not need to be elaborate. A typed codebase, migrations in the repository, environment-based configuration, a documented deploy path, and a test on the one workflow that matters is a good standard for an MVP. What you are listening for is whether the vendor has thought about the handover at all. Many have not, and their projects arrive on my desk a year later as repairs, which is where the material for this guide on fixing broken apps came from.

Ownership belongs in the contract in plain words: all source code and all accounts, hosting, domains, third-party services, are yours, transferred on final payment. The repository should be in your organization from the first week, not transferred at the end.

What happens after launch?

Launch is where the real product work starts, and the vendor's plan for that period tells you how they think about the engagement. Ask what support is included after launch, for how long, and at what price after that. Ask what the path is if you want ongoing work: a retainer, a monthly arrangement, or a new project each time.

SortedLaunch includes launch and two weeks of post-launch fixes in every project, and offers Regular Work at $3,000 per month that can be paused or cancelled anytime. The specific numbers matter less than the existence of a clear answer. A vendor who has not thought about the month after launch has not thought about your product as something that will be used.

Which signals should end the conversation?

Some answers are disqualifying, whatever else is on offer.

  • They will not name the engineer who will write the code, or will not let you speak to them.
  • The code or the hosting accounts stay in the vendor's name.
  • There is no written scope, or the scope has no exclusions.
  • The first demo of working software is more than two weeks away.
  • They cannot provide a founder at a launched product who will take a call.
  • They propose a timeline measured in quarters for a first version.
  • They promise that an MVP will be "complete", "future-proof" or "enterprise-ready". An MVP is none of those things, and a vendor who says otherwise is selling a feeling.

How do you decide between a small agency and a single senior engineer?

Choose by scope shape and by how you like to work. If the first version needs design, web and mobile built at the same time, a small team can run those in parallel and a single engineer cannot. If the first version is one web application with one core workflow, which describes most SaaS MVPs, a single senior engineer with a track record will usually build it faster and cheaper, with nothing lost in translation because there is no translation.

Then consider yourself. Some founders want a project manager who turns their messages into tickets and shields them from the details. Others want to talk to the engineer and see the staging build every day. The first kind will be happier with an agency. The second kind will be frustrated by one.

Whichever you choose, run the questions in this guide before you sign, write the answers down, and put the important ones in the contract. The founders who have the smoothest builds are the ones who treated the hire like an engineering decision rather than a purchase, and the questions above are how an engineer would make it. Frequently asked questions

How much does an MVP development agency cost?

Agency quotes vary widely with team size and location, and a quote that includes a project manager, a designer and several developers will be priced accordingly. Ask for a fixed price for a fixed scope rather than an hourly estimate. SortedLaunch, as a comparison point, builds full projects from $3,500 at a fixed price agreed before work starts.

How long should an MVP take to build?

A SaaS MVP with auth, billing and one core workflow should take weeks, not months. SortedLaunch builds SaaS MVPs in 3 to 6 weeks, AI products in 2 to 4 weeks, mobile apps in about 4 weeks and Chrome extensions in 2 to 3 weeks. A quote measured in quarters usually means the scope is too large for an MVP.

Should I hire an agency or a freelancer for my MVP?

A single senior engineer gives you direct access to the person writing the code and a lower price, at the cost of capacity. An agency gives you capacity and a bench if someone leaves, at the cost of layers between you and the work. For a first version with one core workflow, a senior individual is usually the better buy. For a product that needs design, mobile and web at the same time, a small team may fit better.

Do I own the code when the agency is done?

You should, and the contract should say so in plain words: full ownership of the source code and all accounts, transferred on final payment. Walk away from any vendor that keeps the code in their own repositories or hosts the product on accounts you cannot access.

What should an MVP contract include?

A written scope with what is in and what is out, a fixed price or a capped budget, a delivery date, a demo cadence, who owns the code, how changes are priced, and what support is included after launch. SortedLaunch contracts include a demo every Friday, launch, and two weeks of post-launch fixes.
